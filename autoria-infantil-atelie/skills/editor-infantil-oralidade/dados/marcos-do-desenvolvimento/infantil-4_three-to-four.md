Developmental Milestones

Three to Four Years | v1

## Understanding the tables

The characteristics in these tables describe behaviours that many children may demonstrate within an approximate age range. Development does not occur at exactly the same rate or in the same sequence for every child. Educators should consider each child’s individual strengths, experiences, abilities, interests, family background, and cultural context.

These tables support observation and teaching decisions. They are not screening or diagnostic tools. Educators should not diagnose or label children. Concerns about development, persistent difficulties, or the loss of previously acquired skills should be documented and shared through the setting’s established referral procedures.

The distinction between broad developmental guidance and formal screening is important because milestone lists support surveillance and conversation rather than diagnosis (Zubler et al., 2022).

## The Three- to Four-Year-Old Child

Around age four, children are often more confident, imaginative, and socially engaged. They communicate in longer sentences, remember familiar experiences, and become more involved in pretend and cooperative play. Their attention, coordination, and independence are developing, but they still need clear routines, modelling, manageable instructions, and adult support to regulate emotions and navigate social situations (CDC, 2021b; NAEYC, 2020).

### Physical development at three to four years

| Approximate age | What many children may demonstrate by this age | What this may mean | Teaching practices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three | Uses a fork; puts on some loose clothing; strings large items together. | Self-care and hand–eye coordination are becoming more purposeful, although tasks may remain slow and inconsistent. | Include dressing, serving, tidying, threading, construction, and tool use in ordinary routines. Model each step and provide assistance only where needed. |
| Three | Participates confidently in active movement but may still misjudge speed, space, height, or other children’s movements. | Physical confidence may develop more quickly than judgement and impulse control. | Provide active play with clear boundaries and close supervision. Use simple safety language and allow manageable physical challenge. |
| Four | Catches a large ball most of the time. | Tracking, timing, bilateral coordination, and body control are improving. | Use large, soft balls for rolling, throwing, kicking, and catching. Adjust distance and speed so children experience success. |
| Four | Serves food or pours water with adult supervision and unbuttons some buttons. | Coordination and self-care independence are becoming more refined. | Build real responsibilities into meals and routines. Use child-sized containers and allow minor spills as part of learning. |
| Four | Holds a crayon or pencil between the fingers and thumb rather than using a full-fist grip. | A more functional grasp may be emerging, although grip and hand preference may still vary. | Provide frequent drawing, painting, construction, playdough, and tool-use experiences. Model a comfortable grip without forcing hand position or demanding neatness. |
| Three to five | Enjoys running, climbing, jumping, dancing, carrying, building, and moving in varied ways. | Physical activity supports health, attention, confidence, self-regulation, and learning across other domains. | Integrate movement throughout the day rather than limiting it to a single break. Offer both energetic and calming forms of movement. |

CDC basis: three-year physical indicators. Four-year physical indicators.

### Cognitive development at three to four years

| Approximate age | What many children may demonstrate by this age | What this may mean | Teaching practices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three | Draws a circle after watching an adult demonstrate it. | Children are increasingly able to imitate a visual-motor action and represent a simple shape. | Model drawing as part of meaningful activity without requiring exact copies. Provide large surfaces and varied drawing tools. |
| Three | Avoids touching a hot object after an adult warning. | Children are beginning to connect familiar warnings with immediate consequences, but adult supervision remains essential. | Use brief, concrete safety explanations and consistent boundaries. Do not assume that verbal knowledge will always control behaviour. |
| Four | Names some colours. | Children are increasingly noticing, remembering, and labelling visual features. | Discuss colour naturally during art, dressing, sorting, outdoor exploration, and construction rather than relying mainly on flashcard drills. |
| Four | Tells what comes next in a familiar story. | Memory, prediction, sequencing, and story understanding are developing. | Re-read favourite books, pause before familiar events, and invite predictions without requiring one correct answer. |
| Four | Draws a person with three or more body parts. | Representation, body knowledge, planning, and fine-motor control are becoming more complex. | Offer open-ended drawing and opportunities to talk about children’s creations. Avoid adult-drawn templates or expectations that all pictures look alike. |

CDC basis: three-year cognitive indicators. Four-year cognitive indicators.

### Language and communication at three to four years

| Approximate age | What many children may demonstrate by this age | What this may mean | Teaching practices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three | Participates in a conversation containing at least two back-and-forth exchanges. | Children are increasingly able to listen, respond, and maintain a shared topic briefly. | Pause after speaking, follow the child’s topic, and use comments as well as questions to extend conversation. |
| Three | Asks “who,” “what,” “where,” or “why” questions. | Questions help children gather information, maintain interaction, and understand events. | Respond seriously to questions, model curiosity, and investigate answers together when appropriate. |
| Three | Identifies an action occurring in a picture or book. | Children are connecting images, verbs, experience, and spoken language. | Discuss what characters are doing and invite children to act out actions or connect them to personal experience. |
| Three | Says their first name when asked and is understood by familiar and unfamiliar listeners most of the time. | Speech, identity language, and conversational confidence are developing, although some pronunciation differences may remain. | Listen patiently, avoid repeated public correction, and model words naturally in conversation. Document persistent concerns rather than diagnosing them. |
| Four | Uses sentences containing four or more words. | Grammar and the ability to combine ideas are becoming more complex. | Model varied sentence structures and extend children’s statements without demanding that they repeat the adult version. |
| Four | Recalls words from familiar songs, stories, or nursery rhymes. | Rhythm, repetition, memory, and language are working together. | Repeat songs, chants, poems, and stories, allowing children to complete predictable lines or invent alternatives. |
| Four | Talks about at least one event from their day. | Children are beginning to organise and communicate personal experiences. | Use open invitations such as “Tell me about what you built” rather than repeatedly asking broad questions such as “What did you do today?” |
| Four | Answers simple questions about what familiar objects are used for. | Vocabulary is increasingly organised around function, category, and everyday knowledge. | Explore real objects and discuss their purposes during play and routines. Accept sensible alternative uses during imaginative play. |

CDC basis: three-year language indicators. Four-year language indicators.

### Social and emotional development at three to four years

| Approximate age | What many children may demonstrate by this age | What this may mean | Teaching practices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three | Calms within approximately ten minutes after a caregiver leaves in a familiar childcare setting. | Children are increasingly able to use familiar adults, routines, and activities to recover from separation. | Use a predictable arrival routine, acknowledge the child’s feelings, and support connection with a familiar adult or activity. Do not shame prolonged distress. |
| Three | Notices other children and joins them in play. | Interest in shared activity is increasing, although entry into play, negotiation, sharing, and conflict resolution may still require adult support. | Model simple entry phrases, help children find a role, and remain nearby to scaffold interaction without directing every detail. |
| Four | Takes on roles during pretend play, such as a family member, animal, worker, or imaginary character. | Pretend play supports symbolic thought, language, perspective-taking, and social understanding. | Provide flexible props and time for sustained role-play. Join as a co-player when helpful, then reduce support as children develop the play themselves. |
| Four | Asks to join other children’s play. | Children are becoming more able to communicate social intentions directly. | Teach and model several respectful ways to enter play. Help the group create an available role rather than forcing immediate inclusion without support. |
| Four | Comforts someone who is hurt or sad. | Awareness of others’ emotions and prosocial responses are becoming more evident. | Name emotions, model caring actions, and acknowledge genuine efforts to help. Do not require affection such as hugging. |
| Four | Enjoys being a helper. | Meaningful participation can strengthen belonging, competence, identity, and responsibility. | Offer real classroom responsibilities rather than artificial rewards. Rotate opportunities without attaching fixed labels such as “good helper.” |
| Four | Changes behaviour according to the setting and increasingly avoids obvious dangers. | Children are learning that expectations and risks vary across environments, though judgement remains incomplete. | Explain expectations positively, model safe behaviour, and provide manageable opportunities to practise judgement. Continue close supervision. |
| Three to five | Experiences conflicts, disappointment, frustration, excitement, and changing friendships. | Social-emotional competence develops through repeated supported experience, not through adult explanation alone. | Coach children through real situations: name the problem, hear each perspective, help generate solutions, and gradually reduce adult support. |

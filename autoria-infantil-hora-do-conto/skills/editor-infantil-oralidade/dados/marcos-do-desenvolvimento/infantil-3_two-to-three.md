Developmental Milestones

Two to Three Years | v1

## Understanding the tables

The characteristics in these tables describe behaviours that many children may demonstrate within an approximate age range. Development does not occur at exactly the same rate or in the same sequence for every child. Educators should consider each child’s individual strengths, experiences, abilities, interests, family background, and cultural context.

These tables support observation and teaching decisions. They are not screening or diagnostic tools. Educators should not diagnose or label children. Concerns about development, persistent difficulties, or the loss of previously acquired skills should be documented and shared through the setting’s established referral procedures.

The distinction between broad developmental guidance and formal screening is important because milestone lists support surveillance and conversation rather than diagnosis (Zubler et al., 2022).

## The Two- to Three-Year-Old Child

Around age three, children are active, curious, and increasingly interested in communicating and joining other children. They learn through movement, imitation, repetition, conversation, play, and hands-on exploration. Their independence is growing, but they still need considerable adult support with instructions, waiting, sharing, transitions, and emotional regulation. Participation may be brief, physical, or changeable rather than quiet and sustained (CDC, 2021a; NAEYC, 2020).

### Physical development at two to three years

| What many children may demonstrate | What this may mean | Teaching practices |
|---|---|---|
| Increasing participation in self-care routines, such as using utensils, drinking from a cup, washing hands, carrying belongings, or helping with dressing. | Children are developing independence but may still need time, reminders, physical assistance, or emotional support. | Build self-care into the daily routine. Break tasks into small steps, model what to do, and allow children time to try before helping. Avoid taking over simply because an adult can complete the task faster. |
| Growing confidence in walking, running, climbing, kicking, pushing, pulling, and attempting to jump. | Balance, strength, spatial awareness, and coordination are developing through repeated movement. | Provide daily opportunities for safe indoor and outdoor movement. Use open-ended equipment such as low climbing structures, balls, tunnels, cushions, and push-and-pull toys. |
| Frequent changes of position during play, including sitting, squatting, standing, reaching, carrying, and moving between activities. | Movement supports physical development, attention, exploration, and regulation. Remaining still for long periods may be difficult. | Avoid lengthy seated activities. Allow children to listen, sing, talk, and explore while standing, moving, or changing position. |
| Moving in response to music through clapping, swaying, marching, spinning, or jumping. | Music supports rhythm, coordination, memory, participation, and enjoyment. | Use songs and movement throughout the day. Model simple actions while allowing children to participate in their own way rather than requiring accurate performance. |
| Increasing control when scribbling, turning pages, stacking objects, manipulating playdough, or using simple tools. | Fine-motor strength and coordination are emerging, but precision and stamina remain limited. | Offer chunky crayons, paintbrushes, playdough, blocks, puzzles, posting activities, and supervised cutting experiences. Prioritise open-ended mark-making and creation over neat or identical finished products. |

### Cognitive development at two to three years

| What many children may demonstrate | What this may mean | Teaching practices |
|---|---|---|
| Exploring how objects work through pushing, dropping, filling, emptying, stacking, rolling, opening, and closing. | Children learn through cause and effect, repetition, trial and error, and sensory feedback. | Provide materials that respond visibly to children’s actions. Allow repeated experimentation before demonstrating a solution. |
| Matching, grouping, or sorting familiar objects according to noticeable features. | Children are beginning to recognise relationships, similarities, differences, and simple categories. | Include informal sorting and matching in play and routines. Talk about colour, shape, size, texture, and function without turning every activity into a test. |
| Following a short, familiar direction with gesture, modelling, or repetition. | Working memory and receptive language are developing, but lengthy verbal instructions may be difficult to retain. | Give one clear direction at a time. Use gestures, demonstrations, pictures, or objects to show what is expected. |
| Focusing for short periods during self-chosen or highly engaging play. | Attention depends strongly on interest, familiarity, emotional state, sensory needs, and the level of challenge. | Plan brief adult-led experiences and longer periods of uninterrupted play. Avoid using a fixed “attention span” as a measure of ability or behaviour. |
| Copying familiar actions and beginning to use objects symbolically in pretend play. | Imitation and emerging symbolic thought help children make sense of everyday experiences. | Model actions and language, provide familiar pretend-play materials, and join children’s play sensitively without controlling the storyline. |
| Anticipating familiar routines, songs, or repeated parts of stories. | Predictability supports memory, security, participation, and understanding. | Keep important routines consistent and use repeated stories, songs, visual cues, and transition signals. Prepare children calmly when routines need to change. |

### Language and communication at two to three years

| What many children may demonstrate | What this may mean | Teaching practices |
|---|---|---|
| Communicating through a combination of words, short phrases, facial expressions, gestures, movement, and tone of voice. | Communication ability may be greater than spoken sentence length suggests. Children may understand more than they can express clearly. | Respond to the child’s intended meaning. Model a slightly longer phrase without requiring the child to repeat it; for example, respond to “more juice” with “You would like more juice.” |
| Rapidly learning words connected to familiar people, objects, actions, and routines. | Vocabulary develops most effectively when language is connected to meaningful experience. | Narrate play and routines using clear, natural language. Repeat useful words in different situations rather than presenting long lists of isolated vocabulary. |
| Using language, gesture, or expression to ask for help, protest, choose, comment, or show curiosity. | Communication is becoming an important tool for independence and emotional expression. | Give children time to communicate. Offer simple choices and model functional phrases such as “Help me,” “My turn,” “All done,” and “Can I have it?” |
| Joining in with familiar songs, actions, rhymes, repeated words, or sound effects. | Rhythm and repetition support listening, anticipation, memory, speech, and participation. | Repeat familiar songs and rhymes regularly. Pause at predictable points so children can contribute a sound, action, or word. |
| Enjoying short stories with clear pictures, repetition, familiar events, and expressive delivery. | Shared reading supports language, attention, relationships, imagination, and early literacy. | Read interactively. Point, comment, imitate sounds, and follow children’s interests. It is not necessary to explain or question every page. |

### Social and emotional development at two to three years

| What many children may demonstrate | What this may mean | Teaching practices |
|---|---|---|
| Strong preferences, frequent use of “no,” resistance to adult help, or a desire to complete tasks independently. | These behaviours commonly reflect growing autonomy and a developing sense of self. | Offer limited, genuine choices and allow safe independence. Maintain clear boundaries without interpreting every refusal as defiance. |
| Playing alongside other children, watching them, copying them, and gradually beginning to join their play. | Parallel play remains common, while interest in peers and shared activity is developing. | Provide enough materials and space for side-by-side play. Model simple ways to enter play without requiring sustained cooperation. |
| Difficulty sharing, waiting, or taking turns consistently. | Impulse control, perspective-taking, and understanding of time are still developing. | Do not demand immediate sharing. Use duplicates where possible, support brief turns, name what is happening, and help children wait with a concrete cue or alternative activity. |
| Distress during separation or transition, followed by gradual settling with support. | Young children rely on predictable relationships and routines to feel secure. | Use consistent arrival and goodbye routines. Acknowledge feelings, provide reassurance, and help the child connect with a familiar person or activity. |
| Strong emotional reactions that may be expressed physically or through crying, shouting, withdrawal, or refusal. | Children still depend on adults for co-regulation and may not yet have words or strategies for intense feelings. | Stay calm, keep everyone safe, name the feeling, reduce unnecessary language, and support recovery before discussing what happened. |
| Beginning to notice others’ emotions or offer simple comfort. | Empathy is emerging but remains inconsistent and strongly supported by adult modelling. | Describe feelings and model caring responses: “She is sad. Let’s see how we can help.” Avoid forcing apologies that the child does not yet understand. |
| Frequently seeking adult attention, proximity, or reassurance. | Warm, responsive relationships provide the security children need to explore and learn. | Give regular positive attention before behaviour escalates. Notice effort, communicate warmly, and remain emotionally available during play and routines. |
